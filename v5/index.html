The Machine Room

The current work

Two years of teaching generative tooling to carry an idea from first sketch to finished product without losing the hand that made it. Not prompts typed into somebody else's website: a stack that runs on my own machine, models I have trained myself, and pipelines built to produce the same answer twice.

A model will happily give you something plausible and wrong. Knowing which is which is still the job.

I came to it as an artist, which is the part that matters. Thirty years of drawing means I can tell at a glance when a hand has six fingers, when a light source has wandered between two shots, and when a thing is merely impressive rather than right.

The rest is engineering: reference sheets, trained weights, automated gates, and the patience to run a shot again. It is the same craft it always was. The brush is just faster, and it argues back.

Where I stop

Client material does not go to a third-party service. A real person's likeness does not get generated without their say-so. And nothing ships that I have not looked at frame by frame — a tool that makes a thousand images still needs somebody who can say which one is right.

Specification — what runs here
Machine Apple silicon, local and offline. No upload, no third party, no client data leaving the building.
Front ends ComfyUI, InvokeAI and AUTOMATIC1111 over one shared model library.
Image Flux.1 and Flux.2, SDXL, Qwen-Image, Z-Image, SD 1.5 — chosen per shot, not per habit.
Video Image-to-video and restyle passes, driven shot by shot rather than clip by clip.
Training LoRAs trained on my own reference sheets, so a character stays the same character across hundreds of frames.
Tooling A desktop app I wrote: finds and installs models from Civitai and Hugging Face, queues restyle and video jobs, and keeps the kit portable on an external drive.
In service The Designery, 2024—25: renders turned around inside a meeting, and client walkthroughs in VR. Cirrus Aircraft, 2025—now: R&D inside a regulated training programme.

The Record

All six references

I started in 3D in 1998, modelling characters for films that went out on national television. That taught me to think in space before I think in layers — and it is still how I plan a frame, a lesson or a page.

Since then: nine years at Jellyvision turning benefits enrolment into something watchable, a contract month at Jackbox drawing two hundred props, three years at Tynker running graphics for a product children actually used. Different rooms, same job — make the thing clear, then make it worth looking at.

The newest part of the practice is the generative one, and I treat it the way I treated 3D in 1998: learn the machine properly, then use it to do the work I already knew how to do, faster and at a scale I could not reach alone.
